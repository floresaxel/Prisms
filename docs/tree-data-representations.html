Ways to show a tree

Most people reach for an org chart or an indented list. There are a dozen better-fitting ways to draw hierarchical data for human readers, and the right one depends on what the reader needs to do.

Match the form to the questionFinding, weighing, explaining, planning and diagnosing each have a different best shape.
Size is the big missing pieceOrg charts and outlines show structure but not weight. Icicle charts, sunbursts and treemaps add it.
Many strong forms are not "trees"Fishbones, brackets, hourglasses and skill trees are trees in disguise, tuned for one job.

1. The familiar baselines

These are what most readers already know. They set the bar the other forms have to beat.

Baseline

Org chart

Shows who reports to whom at a glance.

Wide trees sprawl sideways, and nothing shows size or value.

Baseline

Indented outline

Dense, scannable, easy to edit, handles deep nesting.

No overview. You cannot see the shape or compare branches.

Baseline

Mind map

Memorable, good for brainstorming around one idea.

Turns messy past roughly 50 nodes, and the layout is subjective.

Baseline

File-explorer tree

Familiar and collapsible.

You only see the part you have expanded.

2. Less common hierarchical forms

These add something the baselines lack: weight, containment, or a drill-down path that stays visible.

Space-filling

Icicle chart

Rectangles stacked by depth, with width proportional to value.

You see where the weight sits and how deep each branch goes.

Narrow slices are unreadable, so labels need hover.

Best for: budgets, time spent, "what is eating my effort?"
Space-filling

Sunburst

An icicle chart wrapped into rings: the whole at the centre, the detail at the rim.

Shows part-to-whole across two to four levels.

Outer rings are hard to compare by angle.

Best for: composition breakdowns
Space-filling

Treemap

Nested rectangles sized by value.

The most efficient use of space for thousands of weighted leaves.

The hierarchy is hard to see, and beyond three levels it turns to mush.

Best for: disk usage, portfolios, market maps
Containment

Circle packing

Child circles nested inside their parent.

"Belongs to" reads instantly, even to non-technical viewers.

Wastes about a fifth of the space, and area is hard to judge.

Best for: small, friendly overviews
Drill-down

Miller columns

Each selection opens its children in a new column to the right, as in Finder's column view.

Drilling down while the whole path stays visible.

One path at a time and no global view.

Best for: browsing deep taxonomies
Distance

Dendrogram

Branch length means "how different".

Shows similarity and clusters directly.

The length is only meaningful for similarity data.

Best for: clustering, evolution, taxonomy

3. Useful forms you may not think of as trees

Each of these is a hierarchy shaped for one specific job.

Decide

Issue / logic tree

A question splits into branches that do not overlap and together cover everything (MECE).

Disciplined, so gaps and overlaps show up.

Best for: decisions, root-cause and strategy work
Diagnose

Fishbone (Ishikawa)

Causes drawn as ribs feeding one effect on the right.

Puts the problem at the root and the causes in categories.

Best for: "why did this fail?"
Plan

Work breakdown + Gantt

Hierarchy on the left, timeline on the same rows.

Structure and schedule in one view.

Best for: project and task hierarchies
Context

Hourglass chart

One person at the centre, ancestors above and descendants below.

Far better than a plain family tree for "show me this person in context".

Best for: genealogy and focus-plus-neighbours views
Outcome

Tournament bracket

A tree read from the leaves to the root.

The only common form where the leaves are the starting point and the root is the result.

Best for: eliminations, rankings, roll-ups
Prerequisites

Skill / tech tree

Branches are prerequisites, with unlocked and locked nodes visible.

Handles "A needs B and C" better than a strict tree.

Best for: learning paths, dependencies, progression
Containment

Nested boxes (Euler)

Pure containment, with no connecting lines.

The most intuitive form for non-technical audiences.

Only works to about three levels.

Cross-links

Metro / transit map

Branches become lines and shared dependencies become interchanges.

Works when the structure is a tree with a few cross-links.

5. Choosing

The reader needs to…Use
Find a thingIndented outline, Miller columns
See where the weight isIcicle chart, treemap, sunburst
Explain or persuadeIssue tree, pyramid-principle outline, nested boxes
Plan workWork breakdown with a Gantt, or a skill tree when there are dependencies
Diagnose a failureFishbone
Explore a huge, unfamiliar treeHyperbolic tree, or a zoomable treemap or sunburst
See one item in contextHourglass chart

6. What this means for Prisms

Recommendation: add an icicle chart next to the existing outline.

Prisms' vision → roadmap → project → task hierarchy is strictly parent-child, which is exactly what an icicle chart draws cleanly. Width can encode effort or remaining work, and colour can encode progress, so it answers "where is my effort going?" without losing the order of the levels. The outline stays the place to edit and find things.
